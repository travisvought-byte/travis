Ohio beekeepers

I keep bees in Marengo. Most of us got into it for the bees and the land, not to run a business. First: register your bees

Every Ohio beekeeper has to register each apiary with the Ohio Department of Agriculture. Do it by June 1 every year, or within 30 days of getting bees or bringing them into Ohio. Registration runs June 1 to May 31.

It's free. Ohio dropped the fee on October 1, 2025. Any spot where your hives sit more than two months in a row counts as an apiary.

Apiary registration form (PDF) · ODA apiary registration page

Your county apiary inspector works for the state and checks colonies for pests and disease. If you plan to sell or give away bees, queens, nucs, comb or used equipment, get your bees inspected first. Your county's inspector is on ODA's inspector list.

Law: Ohio Revised Code 909.02.

Find a club

A club is the fastest way to learn, borrow an extractor and find a mentor. The Ohio State Beekeepers Association speaks for beekeepers at the Statehouse, runs a fall conference and lists 46 local clubs. Dues start at $15 a year.

Selling your honey, legally

Ohio makes this easier than most people think.

  1. Jar your own honey and you need no food license. Ohio exempts a beekeeper who jars honey when at least 75% of it comes from their own hives. There's no hive count or sales cap. (ORC 3715.021)
  2. Label every jar. Your business name and address, the word "Honey" (name a flower, like clover, only if you can back it up), any added ingredients, and the net weight in both ounces and grams. Grams are required for anything sold away from your own place. (ORC 3715.023)
  3. Stores and restaurants can buy it. Ohio law says honey from an exempt beekeeper, labeled this way, is acceptable for a licensed store to sell or a restaurant to use. (ORC 3715.023)
  4. Farmers markets and farm markets need no food license for it. A stand selling only exempt honey is covered, and so are registered farm markets and festivals run by a city, village or township. For other events, ask the local health department. (ORC 3717.22)
  5. Keep it pure. Honey with any added sweetener can't be sold as honey, and a blend can't make "honey" the biggest word on the label. (ORC 3715.38, FDA honey labeling guidance)

Good to know

  • Flavored honeyInfused or flavored honey from an exempt beekeeper is a cottage food: it can be sold to Ohio stores and markets but not out of state, and the label adds "This Product is Home Produced." (OAC 901:3-20-04)
  • Packing other beekeepers' honeyOnce more than 25% of what you jar comes from someone else's hives, you're a food processing business. That means registering with the Ohio Department of Agriculture each year ($50 for a space under 5,000 square feet) and passing an inspection first. (ORC 3715.041)
  • Sales taxHoney is food, so Ohio charges no sales tax on it. Beeswax candles, lip balm and gift items are taxable.
  • An optional state sealAn exempt beekeeper can ask the state to inspect their honey house each year and then use the state's inspection seal on the label. Some buyers like it.
  • Ohio ProudThe state's Ohio-made logo for your label, for products at least half grown or made in Ohio. Membership is $50 a year.

The Ohio Department of Agriculture's honey page has the official details. This page is a plain-language summary, not legal advice.

What honey sells for, per pound

Selling a pail to a packer pays a quarter to a third of what the same honey brings in jars. These are the latest published figures.

  • Bulk, sold to packers$2.23 to $2.70 a poundUSDA National Honey Report, September 2026, U.S.
  • Ohio average, every way it's sold$6.45 a poundUSDA Honey report, 2025 crop, Ohio
  • Wholesale to a store, 1 lb jarAbout $8 a jarBee Culture price report, early 2026, U.S. average
  • Retail, 1 lb jar$8 to $12 from Ohio beekeepers; $12.22 U.S. averageOhio sellers' own pages; Bee Culture, early 2026

Ohio beekeepers made about 765,000 pounds in 2025, down from 1.25 million the year before, and still held 474,000 pounds in December.
