Myths versus reality

Twelve things people repeat about grants, checked against the rules and the data.

Checked against each source on November 27, 2026. Field practice marks experienced practitioners' judgment rather than a rule or a dataset.

“The government gives free grant money to individuals for bills, debt or a new business.”

Myth

Government grants aren't awarded for personal needs. They fund specific programs, research and projects. Offers of personal "free money" from the government are scams. Individuals may qualify for benefits or loans, which are different things.

Sources: USAGov: avoid free money scams; FTC consumer alert, March 2026; Grants.gov: grant-related scams.

“You pay a fee to apply for or receive a federal grant.”

Myth

The government charges no fee to apply for a federal grant, and agencies won't demand payment to give you one. A fee request is the clearest sign of a scam.

Sources: Grants.gov: grant-related scams; FTC consumer alert, March 2026.

“A good grant writer works for a percentage of the award.”

Myth

Professional codes bar it. The Association of Fundraising Professionals' Standard 21 says members shall not accept compensation based on a percentage of contributions, or finder's or contingent fees. Standards for Excellence says the same. Pay a flat or hourly fee.

Sources: AFP Code of Ethical Standards, Standard 21; Standards for Excellence: paying a fundraiser a percentage.

“Grants can't pay for overhead.”

Myth

On federal awards, an organization without a negotiated rate may charge up to 15% of modified total direct costs with no justification, and negotiated rates must be accepted by all agencies. Foundations set their own limits, and many offer general operating support.

Sources: 2 CFR 200.414, indirect costs; EPA: what's new in the 2024 revision to 2 CFR 200.

“Once you have your 501(c)(3), you're set.”

Myth

Fail to file your annual 990, 990-EZ or 990-N for three years in a row and the exemption is revoked automatically. Ohio charities also file an annual report with the Attorney General.

Sources: IRS: automatic revocation for not filing; Ohio Attorney General: starting a charity in Ohio.

“Foundations are where the big charitable money is.”

Myth

In 2025, individuals gave $394.2 billion, about 64% of all giving. Foundations gave $117.15 billion, about 19%, and corporations $43.67 billion, about 7%. Grants matter, but individual donors are most of the money.

Source: Giving USA 2026 press release.

“Federal grants go to whoever knows somebody.”

Myth

Agencies must run an objective merit review against written standards described in the notice, and the notice must publish its criteria. What does favor established groups is the separate risk review, which weighs financial stability, management systems and performance history.

Sources: 2 CFR 200.205, merit review; 2 CFR 200.206, review of risk; 2 CFR 200 Appendix I, what a funding notice must say.

“New organizations can't win federal grants.”

Partly true

No rule bars them. But the risk review looks at history of performance, financial stability and audits, which a new organization can't show yet. Building a record with local and state grants first is the usual path.

Source: 2 CFR 200.206, review of risk.

“Foundations have to give money away, so some of it can come to us.”

Partly true

Private foundations must pay out a minimum distributable amount, based on 5% of their non-charitable-use assets, or face a 30% tax. They decide who gets it, and their own programs and costs can count.

Sources: IRS: private foundation minimum investment return; IRS: private foundation failure to distribute.

“Grant money is free money with no strings.”

Myth

Federal costs must be allowable and documented, records kept three years after the final report, and organizations spending $1,000,000 or more in federal money in a year need a Single Audit. Foundation grants come with their own reports.

Sources: 2 CFR 200.403, allowable costs; 2 CFR 200.334, record retention; EPA: what's new in the 2024 revision to 2 CFR 200.

“Without a 501(c)(3), you can't get grants.”

Partly true

Many funders require one, but a fiscal sponsor that is a 501(c)(3) can receive grants for your project, either as its own program or as a re-grant to you.

Source: Nonprofit Law Blog: do you really know what fiscal sponsorship is?.

“Send the same proposal to as many funders as you can.”

Myth

Funders score fit to their own criteria and priorities, and federal notices publish responsiveness screens that end the review on a mismatch. A tailored proposal to five well-qualified funders beats a generic one to fifty. Field practice

Source: 2 CFR 200 Appendix I, what a funding notice must say.
