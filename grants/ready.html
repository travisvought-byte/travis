Get ready before you ask

The first read of your application is not your story. It's whether you exist, are in good standing and can be trusted with money.

Funders and federal agencies check status records that anyone can look up. Fix these first, in this order. Each one takes days to weeks, and none can be rushed the week a deadline lands.

Legal and tax status

  • A legal entity. In Ohio, a nonprofit corporation is formed with the Secretary of State under Ohio Revised Code chapter 1702 (or 1745 for unincorporated associations). Ohio AG overview
  • An IRS determination letter. Most foundations grant only to 501(c)(3) public charities, or to others with extra paperwork. Your status and whether donors can deduct gifts to you show up in the IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search, which draws on Publication 78 data and the determination letters. IRS search
  • No 501(c)(3) yet? You can still take grant money through a fiscal sponsor. See No 501(c)(3)?

Annual filings: the quiet deal-breaker

Every year your organization files a Form 990, 990-EZ or 990-N with the IRS. Organizations whose gross receipts are normally $50,000 or less can file the short 990-N e-Postcard; above that it's the 990-EZ or full 990.

Miss three years in a row and your exemption is revoked automatically. The IRS publishes the list of revoked organizations, and anyone can search it.

IRS automatic revocation · Who files the 990-N

Your 990s are public. Anyone, including every program officer, can read years of them free on ProPublica's Nonprofit Explorer, which holds millions of filings back to 2001. Read your own the way a funder would: revenue trend, program spending, officer pay, and whether the mission on page one matches what you're asking money for. Field practice

Sources: IRS: who must file Form 990-N; IRS: automatic revocation for not filing; ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer.

Ohio Attorney General registration

Ohio charities, and any organization soliciting for a charitable purpose in Ohio, must register with the Ohio Attorney General, whether or not they're tax-exempt. Register within six months of forming. Annual reports are due on the 15th day of the fifth month after your fiscal year ends, matching an IRS extension if you take one. Fees run on a sliding scale shown at filing. Groups organized exclusively for religious purposes are the most common exemption.

Other states have their own charity registration offices; most sit in the attorney general's or secretary of state's office. If you raise money in a state, check its rule before you solicit there. Field practice

Sources: Ohio Attorney General: starting a charity in Ohio; Ohio Attorney General: online registration information sheet.

Books, board and outcomes

None of these is a single legal rule; they're what a reviewer looks for when judging whether you can run the money. Field practice

  • A budget for the organization, not only the project. A project budget with no organizational budget behind it reads as a group that hasn't planned past this grant.
  • Clean, separate books. A dedicated bank account, a chart of accounts that can track restricted money by grant, and monthly statements someone other than the bookkeeper reads.
  • A working board. Minutes, a conflict of interest policy (the Form 990 asks whether you have one) and members who aren't all related or all staff.
  • Outcome data you already collect. Counts of who you served and what changed for them, kept the same way year to year. A new program can borrow evidence from published research; an existing one should have its own numbers.
  • Letters of support and partners lined up early. They take weeks to collect and the best partners are asked by everyone.

Federal-ready, before any deadline

  • Register in SAM.gov to get a Unique Entity ID. The UEI comes about a day after you enter your E-Business Point of Contact; full SAM processing takes 7 to 10 business days after everything is entered. It costs nothing.
  • Register in Grants.gov, same day, and have your E-Business Point of Contact assign who can submit.
  • Keep SAM active. Federal rules require an active, current registration the whole time an application is under consideration or an award is open, renewed at least once a year.
  • Get your written systems in order. Federal agencies review the financial stability, management systems, performance history and audit findings of every applicant before an award.

Sources: Grants.gov: organization registration; 2 CFR 25.200, keeping SAM.gov registration active; 2 CFR 200.206, review of risk.

Check yourself the way a funder will

  1. Search your name and EIN in the IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search: are you listed, and not on the revocation list?
  2. Pull your last three returns on Nonprofit Explorer: do they tell a consistent story?
  3. Look yourself up in the Ohio Attorney General's charity search: is this year's annual report filed?
  4. For federal money, sign in to SAM.gov: is the registration active, and when does it expire?
